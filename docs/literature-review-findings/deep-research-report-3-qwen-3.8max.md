# Auditing Novelty in Quadrotor Adaptive Control: A Literature Review on Priors, Normalization, and Robust Adaptation

## Scenario-Conditioned Priors in Adaptive Quadrotor Control

The concept of employing scenario-conditioned priors represents a significant step towards creating more intelligent and efficient adaptive controllers for quadrotors operating in complex and variable environments [[316](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1742-6596/1948/1/012109/pdf)]. The central idea is to leverage prior knowledge about likely operational scenarios to initialize or guide the adaptation process, thereby accelerating learning and improving performance. This approach moves beyond traditional model reference adaptive control (MRAC), which often relies on a single set of parameters that must learn from scratch in response to disturbances [[2](https://www.mathworks.com/help/slcontrol/ug/quadrotor-control-using-model-reference-adaptive-control.html), [18](https://www.mdpi.com/2079-3197/11/8/163)]. The research question of whether such a mechanism exists with formal guarantees is of highest priority, as its existence would fundamentally shape the novelty of any new contribution. The provided literature reveals several distinct but related approaches to this problem, including Multiple-Model Adaptive Control (MMAC), Gaussian Process-based MRAC (GP-MRAC), and the highly relevant case of Neural-Fly. Each of these methods implements a form of scenario conditioning, though through different mathematical frameworks and with varying degrees of formal justification.

Multiple-Model Adaptive Control (MMAC) is a classical paradigm where a bank of fixed controllers is designed offline to cover a range of anticipated plant uncertainties or operating modes [[28](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/299747127_Robust_Adaptive_Control)]. An identifier module monitors the system's behavior online and selects the most appropriate controller from the bank based on current conditions [[60](http://www.dem.ist.utl.pt/poliveira/Invest/AST_PO21.pdf)]. This method directly embodies the principle of scenario conditioning through a discrete set of pre-defined models. In the context of quadrotors, one study explicitly proposes an MMAC architecture for controlling a vehicle transporting an unknown constant load, distinguishing between height and yaw control modules [[60](http://www.dem.ist.utl.pt/poliveira/Invest/AST_PO21.pdf)]. Another source mentions the application of a multiple-model solution for a quadrotor, indicating that this technique has been explored for UAVs [[60](http://www.dem.ist.utl.pt/poliveira/Invest/AST_PO21.pdf)]. The primary advantage of MMAC is its conceptual simplicity and the potential for strong theoretical guarantees associated with each individual model. However, challenges arise during the transient period when switching between controllers, as ensuring the stability of the overall switched system can be non-trivial [[15](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.08676)]. While the literature confirms the application of MMAC to quadrotors, the provided sources do not offer explicit details on the formal stability guarantees for these specific implementations, focusing instead on the proposed architecture [[60](http://www.dem.ist.utl.pt/poliveira/Invest/AST_PO21.pdf)].

A more modern and flexible approach to handling uncertainty without predefined scenarios is offered by Gaussian Process Model Reference Adaptive Control (GP-MRAC). This Bayesian nonparametric method treats the system uncertainty as a stochastic process, allowing the controller to adapt online without requiring prior knowledge of the operating domain of the disturbance [[56](https://arc.aiaa.org/doi/10.2514/1.I010190), [96](https://dspace.mit.edu/bitstreams/48360f5e-38e6-4dcd-a8d9-495f247afd74/download)]. By using stochastic stability arguments, GP-MRAC can guarantee good closed-loop performance even in the face of unforeseen conditions [[57](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/348360461_Modified_Gaussian_process_regression_based_adaptive_control_for_quadrotors), [145](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/259740896_Nonparametric_Adaptive_Control_using_Gaussian_Processes)]. This provides a powerful form of robustness, as the controller continuously learns a probabilistic model of the environment. While this is not scenario conditioning in the discrete sense of MMAC, it can be viewed as a continuous form of prior selection, where the GP itself acts as a flexible prior over the space of possible uncertainties. Its theoretical foundation rests on stochastic stability, which ensures boundedness of the closed-loop signals, making it a compelling alternative for applications where the full spectrum of potential scenarios is difficult to anticipate [[97](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1270963825002664)]. The main challenge lies in the computational complexity of maintaining and updating the GP model in real-time, especially for high-dimensional systems.

The most sophisticated and empirically validated example of rapid, scenario-based adaptation found in the literature is Neural-Fly [[50](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/360388122_Neural-Fly_enables_rapid_learning_for_agile_flight_in_strong_winds), [144](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Neural-Fly-enables-rapid-learning-for-agile-flight-O'Connell-Shi/91e6d31e3bb634007dbc3abc3d84da01412fea17)]. Developed at Caltech, Neural-Fly is a learning-based framework designed for agile flight in strong and unpredictable winds [[51](https://www.grepow.com/industry-news/the-neural-fly-drone-from-caltech-can-fly-solidly-in-high-winds.html), [99](https://oamonitor.ireland.openaire.eu/national/search/publication?pid=10.1126%2Fscirobotics.abm6597)]. It operates on two key insights: first, aerodynamic effects in different wind conditions share a common underlying representation; second, the variations between these conditions lie in a low-dimensional space [[49](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.06908)]. To exploit this, Neural-Fly employs a domain adversarially invariant meta-learning algorithm to learn this shared representation from just 12 minutes of flight data [[49](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.06908), [53](https://github.com/aerorobotics/neural-fly)]. Once learned, a composite adaptation law updates a small set of linear coefficients to mix the basis elements of this representation, enabling the controller to rapidly adapt to new, unseen wind scenarios [[49](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.06908)]. This mechanism constitutes a highly effective form of scenario conditioning, where the "scenarios" are continuous changes in wind vector. Crucially, Neural-Fly is not only an empirical success but also a theoretically grounded one. The framework admits formal guarantees of boundedness, convergence, and input-to-state stability [[50](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/360388122_Neural-Fly_enables_rapid_learning_for_agile_flight_in_strong_winds)]. One source even claims exponential stability, which implies robustness guarantees against challenging wind conditions [[360](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35507683/)]. Empirical validation was conducted in wind tunnel tests with speeds up to 43.6 km/h (12.1 m/s), where Neural-Fly demonstrated substantially smaller tracking errors compared to state-of-the-art nonlinear and adaptive controllers [[49](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.06908)]. Furthermore, it showed generalization capabilities, performing effectively in outdoor flights with only onboard sensors and transferring across different drone platforms with minimal performance degradation [[49](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.06908)]. The existence of Neural-Fly demonstrates that the core concept of scenario-conditioned adaptation with strong theoretical backing is not only feasible but has been successfully implemented and validated in a demanding quadrotor application.

In summary, the answer to the highest-risk question is a clear "yes." The concept of scenario-conditioned priors is well-established in the literature, with Neural-Fly representing the most advanced and rigorously validated implementation for quadrotors. Any new contribution in this area cannot claim novelty for the core idea. Instead, its value must be derived from offering a superior mechanism for prior selection, perhaps through faster convergence, lower computational cost, stronger theoretical guarantees under specific assumptions, or a more efficient method for determining the active prior. The existence of Neural-Fly's formal stability proofs sets a high bar for any new theory, while its practical success provides a formidable empirical benchmark.

## Dimensionless Parameterization of Adaptive Weights

The second priority question addresses the originality of normalizing or dimensionless parameterization for adaptive weights in quadrotor control. This technique involves scaling the adaptive parameters to improve numerical properties, ensure boundedness, or facilitate tuning. The user's belief that this may have no precedent warrants a careful investigation of both direct citations in the quadrotor literature and broader concepts in adaptive control theory. An exhaustive search of the provided abstracts and snippets reveals a notable absence of explicit mentions of "dimensionless," "normalized," or "scaling" of adaptive weights specifically within the context of published quadrotor adaptive control papers. Numerous studies detail the design of MRAC systems, derive adaptation laws based on Lyapunov stability theory, and present simulation or flight results, but none cite normalization as a specific component of their methodology [[7](https://www.scribd.com/document/479516614/456), [13](http://www.eleco.org.tr/openconf_2017/modules/request.php?module=oc_proceedings&action=view.php&id=456&file=1/456.pdf&a=Accept+as+Lecture), [46](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/1798434), [58](https://impact.yildiz.edu.tr/articles/a-lyapunov-based-model-reference-adaptive-control-of-a-quadrotor-23786)]. Papers discussing robust modifications like σ-modification or dead-zones also do not mention normalization [[146](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/New-composite-MRAC-with-modification-for-fixed-wing-Lu-Geng/4e7595935b5439a0c9d60042fe6d37f31c0d0db4), [210](https://www.scribd.com/document/924574636/1198679)]. This lack of direct evidence suggests that, within the specific domain of quadrotor control, this is a relatively unexplored technique, lending significant weight to the user's hypothesis of novelty.

However, the concept of normalization is not foreign to the broader field of adaptive control. Foundational texts and tutorials discuss the importance and utility of normalization. One tutorial paper is explicitly titled "Is normalization necessary for stable model reference adaptive control?" and proceeds to provide a systematic demonstration of its role in adaptive control design [[171](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/3031651_Is_normalization_necessary_for_stable_model_reference_adaptive_control)]. This indicates that researchers in the field have long recognized the potential benefits and theoretical implications of parameter normalization. Further evidence comes from a paper on Modified MRAC (M-MRAC) that explicitly incorporates normalization into its formulation [[172](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20190001783/downloads/20190001783.pdf)]. This demonstrates that the idea has been studied and applied in other contexts, even if not prominently featured in the recent quadrotor-specific literature. The use of a normalized adaptive gain is also mentioned in the context of asymptotic linearity of optimal control modification adaptive laws [[78](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/266395000_Asymptotic_Linearity_of_Optimal_Control_Modification_Adaptive_Law_with_Analytical_Stability_Margins)], reinforcing its presence as a known technique in advanced adaptive control design. Therefore, while the user's claim of novelty holds true when narrowly focused on the published quadrotor control literature, it is situated within a larger, albeit less visible, body of work on adaptive control theory.

The primary motivations for normalizing adaptive weights generally fall into three categories: improved numerical conditioning, enhanced stability properties, and facilitation of robustness mechanisms. From a numerical perspective, adaptive systems can suffer from ill-conditioning where parameters evolve at vastly different rates, leading to poor numerical precision and slow convergence. Normalization can help equalize the dynamic range of the parameters, making the adaptation process more numerically stable and predictable. Theoretically, normalization can play a crucial role in stability proofs. By bounding the regressor vector, normalization can help construct Lyapunov functions that lead to stronger stability results, such as uniform ultimate boundedness (UUB) of the parameter error, rather than simple boundedness [[155](https://hankyang.seas.harvard.edu/OptimalControlEstimation/adaptivecontrol.html)]. This is particularly important in the presence of unmodeled dynamics or measurement noise. Finally, in the context of robust adaptive control, normalization can be a prerequisite for certain modifications. For instance, some projection algorithms, which prevent parameter estimates from drifting into unstable regions, are more easily formulated and analyzed when the adaptive gains are normalized [[284](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20100034875/downloads/20100034875.pdf?attachment=true)]. Given these potential benefits, the absence of normalized parameterization in the cited quadrotor literature is a notable gap.

Given the lack of direct precedent in quadrotor control, proposing a dimensionless/normalized adaptive weight parameterization emerges as a strong candidate for a novel contribution. To establish its validity and impact, a future publication would need to clearly articulate the motivation for its application to quadrotors. This could involve highlighting the specific challenges of quadrotor dynamics, such as strong coupling, underactuation, and susceptibility to external disturbances, which might exacerbate numerical issues in the adaptation loop. The theoretical contribution would involve demonstrating how normalization simplifies or strengthens the stability proof for the specific quadrotor controller being designed. For example, one could show that with normalization, a standard MRAC design can satisfy a weaker persistence of excitation (PE) condition, or that it facilitates the incorporation of robustifying terms like σ-modification more cleanly. The empirical contribution would come from comparative simulations or flight tests showing tangible improvements in performance metrics such as tracking accuracy, convergence speed, or robustness to parametric uncertainty compared to a standard, unnormalized adaptive controller. The maturity of this finding is currently at the **Theory** stage, as the concept is established in control theory but its application to quadrotors remains unvalidated in the provided sources. A simulation or flown validation would be required to move it to the next stage.

## Theoretical Validity of σ-Modification with Non-Zero Attractors

The third priority question assesses the theoretical soundness of using σ-modification with a non-zero attractor as a core component of a quadrotor adaptive control system. This mechanism serves as the primary injection channel for incorporating prior information into the adaptive loop. The query hinges on whether adding a non-zero term to the adaptation law—a "non-zero attractor"—compromises the fundamental stability guarantees of the underlying MRAC system. The analysis of the provided literature indicates that this approach is not only theoretically defensible but also a logical extension of well-established robust adaptive control principles. The foundational work on robust adaptive control, particularly the seminal book "Robust Adaptive Control" by Ioannou and Sun, extensively covers σ-modification as a standard technique for enhancing the robustness of adaptive controllers [[91](https://www.e-booksdirectory.com/details.php?ebook=1788), [208](https://books.google.com/books/about/Robust_Adaptive_Control.html?hl=pt-BR&id=TIYqAQAAMAAJ), [249](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/226006113_Robust_adaptive_control_Design_analysis_and_robustness_bounds)]. Its purpose is to address the limitations of standard MRAC, which can exhibit sensitive and poorly damped responses in the presence of unmodeled dynamics and external disturbances [[136](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20090017671/downloads/20090017671.pdf)]. Standard σ-modification achieves this by adding a term proportional to the negative of the estimated parameters (`-σ * θ_hat`) to the adaptation law. This term effectively introduces a high-frequency gain roll-off, which improves the phase margin of the adaptive loop and ensures that the closed-loop system exhibits Uniform Ultimate Boundedness (UUB) of the tracking error, rather than just boundedness [[61](https://www.paperhost.org/proceedings/controls/ECC24/files/0619.pdf), [210](https://www.scribd.com/document/924574636/1198679)].

The introduction of a "non-zero attractor" modifies this standard scheme. Instead of pulling the parameters toward zero, the goal becomes to pull them toward a desired value, `θ_0`. This can be mathematically represented by modifying the adaptation law with a term of the form `-σ * (θ_hat - θ_0)`. If `θ_0` represents a prior estimate or belief about the true plant parameters, this mechanism provides a direct way to inject that prior knowledge into the online adaptation process. This aligns perfectly with the concept of scenario-conditioned priors discussed previously, where `θ_0` could represent the parameter set corresponding to a particular identified operational scenario. The theoretical validity of this approach rests on the fact that the core function of σ-modification—to ensure UUB—is preserved. The addition of the non-zero attractor shifts the equilibrium point of the adaptation dynamics from the origin to `θ_0`, but it does not inherently destabilize the system. As long as a suitable Lyapunov function can be constructed to prove that all signals in the closed loop remain bounded, the fundamental stability guarantee holds. Several sources confirm the use of σ-modification in the context of quadrotor control. For instance, one paper describes a new composite MRAC with modification for a fixed-wing UAV, explicitly using σ-modification to improve robustness against disturbances [[146](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/New-composite-MRAC-with-modification-for-fixed-wing-Lu-Geng/4e7595935b5439a0c9d60042fe6d37f31c0d0db4)]. Another source discusses the use of a sigma modification term in a robust I&I adaptive control design for a class of quadrotors with disturbances [[121](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/347863559_Robust_II_Adaptive_Control_for_a_Class_of_Quadrotors_With_Disturbances)]. These examples demonstrate that the technique is actively researched and applied to quadrotor systems. Furthermore, one study presents a Lyapunov-based controller that uses a sigma-modification-type updating law to adjust controller parameters, providing a template for how such a law can be integrated into a Lyapunov-based design for a quadrotor [[268](https://www.science.gov/topicpages/n/nonlinear+lyapunov-based+control)].

When this mechanism is combined with a scenario-conditioning logic that switches between different priors (and thus different attractor points `θ_0`), the system's stability analysis becomes more complex. The resulting system is a switched system, and its stability must be analyzed using tools from that domain [[15](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.08676)]. The key challenge is to ensure that the system remains stable not only within each mode (with a fixed attractor) but also during the transients caused by switching between modes. Proving global stability for such a system can be demanding. However, the existence of Lyapunov-based proofs for the individual components (the MRAC with a non-zero attractor) provides a solid foundation upon which a more complex proof for the switched system can be built. The maturity of this concept is primarily at the **Theory** stage, as it is a well-founded extension of robust adaptive control theory. While its application to a specific switched quadrotor system would require a custom stability proof, the underlying principles are sound and supported by extensive literature. The inclusion of a non-zero attractor is a plausible and theoretically justified method for incorporating prior information, making it a defensible choice for the user's proposed system. The main theoretical task will be to formally prove the UUB of the overall system, accounting for the switched nature of the prior selection logic.

## Comparative Baselines and Performance Benchmarks

To credibly position any new adaptive control design for quadrotors, it is essential to compare its performance against established baselines. The literature identifies a consistent set of controllers used as benchmarks, which serve as a yardstick for evaluating the efficacy of novel adaptive schemes. The most fundamental baseline is a fixed-gain controller, typically a Linear Quadratic Regulator (LQR) [[103](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.15768v3), [111](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Model-Reference-Adaptive-Control-and-LQR-Control-Ghaffar-Richardson/a7648035ba80df5870018180f4c2b410ee51b4cd)]. An LQR controller is designed for a nominal set of system parameters and provides excellent performance if those parameters remain constant. However, its performance degrades significantly in the presence of parametric uncertainty, modeling errors, or external disturbances—precisely the problems that adaptive control aims to solve [[6](https://zenodo.org/record/1099156/files/10000466.pdf)]. Therefore, comparing a new adaptive controller to an appropriately tuned LQR is the standard starting point for demonstrating its value.

Beyond fixed-gain controllers, the adaptive control community frequently compares against other adaptive control architectures. L1 adaptive control is a prominent example, known for its guaranteed stability margins and has been applied to fault-tolerant control of quadrotors [[10](https://www.sciepublish.com/article/pii/75), [14](https://dspace.lib.cranfield.ac.uk/bitstreams/17341f6a-0c3a-4984-930c-2fbd7e585db6/download), [157](https://dspace.lib.cranfield.ac.uk/bitstreams/e80f0792-e672-49f8-9567-f75cd7fd15e5/download)]. Other MRAC variants, sometimes with specific modifications like e-modification or dead-zones, also serve as relevant baselines [[21](https://www.preprints.org/manuscript/202412.2605), [29](https://commons.erau.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1833&context=edt)]. Additionally, controllers that are popular for agile flight, such as Inertial Navigation and Dynamics Inversion (INDI), are often included in comparisons [[54](https://openreview.net/pdf?id=v0xHsHMFBz)]. Nonlinear Model Predictive Control (NMPC) is another class of advanced controller used for trajectory tracking, valued for its ability to handle constraints and optimize performance over a finite horizon [[163](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/332426391_A_constrained_error-based_MPC_for_path_following_of_quadrotor_with_stability_analysis), [311](https://www.elspub.com/doi/10.55092/aias20250005)]. Finally, robust control techniques like Sliding Mode Control (SMC) are used as baselines due to their inherent insensitivity to matched uncertainties and disturbances [[82](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10846-022-01679-0), [113](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11466434/)].

## Gaps Between Theoretical Guarantees and Practical Application

A recurring and critical theme throughout the literature on adaptive control is the significant gap between the theoretical guarantees provided by stability proofs and the practical realities of implementation and operation. While Lyapunov-based analyses and other mathematical tools can rigorously prove properties like boundedness or uniform ultimate boundedness of the closed-loop system, these assurances often come with idealized assumptions that are difficult to meet in the real world [[138](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20090026333/downloads/20090026333.pdf), [167](http://underactuated.mit.edu/lyapunov.html)]. Understanding these gaps is crucial for developing realistic expectations for any adaptive controller and for navigating the challenges of verification and certification for safety-critical systems. One of the most common discrepancies lies in the nature of the stability guarantee itself. Many proofs for MRAC systems, especially those incorporating robustifying modifications like σ-modification, can only guarantee that the tracking error is ultimately uniformly bounded (UUB) [[61](https://www.paperhost.org/proceedings/controls/ECC24/files/0619.pdf), [354](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20080022957/downloads/20080022957.pdf?attachment=true)]. This means that the error will eventually settle into a small residual band around zero, but the proof provides little information about the size of this band or the speed of convergence to it [[138](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20090026333/downloads/20090026333.pdf)]. In practice, a controller might satisfy this theoretical guarantee but exhibit large overshoots, slow settling times, or a final tracking error that is too large for the intended application. The Lyapunov analysis proves the system is stable, but not necessarily performant in the transient regime.

Another major source of discrepancy is the reliance on assumptions that are difficult or impossible to verify in real-world flight. A classic example is the requirement for the regression matrix to be persistently exciting (PE) [[125](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/13/23/12672)]. This condition ensures that the adaptive law receives enough information over time to correctly estimate the unknown parameters. While PE is a common assumption in theoretical treatments, it is not always satisfied in practice, especially during maneuvers that do not sufficiently excite all system modes. Similarly, some adaptive control designs for linear systems require a positive real or passivity condition on the plant, which may not hold for a quadrotor whose dynamics change significantly with attitude and velocity [[355](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19980237343/downloads/19980237343.pdf)]. Real-world factors such as unmodeled actuator dynamics, sensor noise, quantization effects, and high-frequency vibrations can violate the idealized conditions assumed in the stability proofs, leading to performance degradation or instability that is not predicted by the theory [[29](https://commons.erau.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1833&context=edt)]. The process of designing robust modifications like σ-modification is precisely aimed at mitigating these issues, but it often trades guaranteed performance for guaranteed stability [[210](https://www.scribd.com/document/924574636/1198679)].

These theoretical-practical gaps create substantial challenges for the verification and validation (V&V) and eventual certification of adaptive flight control systems. The very nature of adaptive control, where the controller changes its behavior in response to the plant, makes it difficult to analyze and certify using traditional methods developed for fixed-gain systems [[138](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20090026333/downloads/20090026333.pdf), [240](https://arc.aiaa.org/doi/10.2514/1.G006151)]. Even with a rigorous Lyapunov proof, regulators and certification authorities may demand extensive testing to build confidence in the system's behavior under a wide range of operating conditions [[244](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20100031105/downloads/20100031105.pdf)]. This has led to the development of specialized V&V techniques, including Hardware-in-the-Loop (HIL) simulations, which test the flight hardware against a real-time simulated environment [[220](https://www.unmannedsystemstechnology.com/expo/hardware-in-the-loop-hil/), [221](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20160013264), [282](https://info.pickeringtest.com/hubfs/pil/pil-webinars/MAE%20Fall%202024/MAE-DMC-Webinar-Sept2024-D4-FIN.pdf?hsLang)]. HIL allows for the testing of various failure scenarios and extreme conditions that would be too dangerous or costly to perform in actual flight [[253](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2008ccas.conf..485Y/abstract)]. Ultimately, software and hardware simulation results are compared against actual flight test data to validate the accuracy of the models and the effectiveness of the controller [[254](https://digitalcommons.usu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1109&context=mae_facpub), [281](https://digitalcommons.usu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article)]. This multi-stage process of theory, simulation, and flight testing is essential to bridge the gap between what the mathematics promises and what the system delivers in the air. Acknowledging this gap is a sign of mature understanding in the field and is a necessary part of positioning any new adaptive control design.

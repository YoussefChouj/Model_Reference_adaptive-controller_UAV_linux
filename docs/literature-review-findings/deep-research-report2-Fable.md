# Literature Review for an MRAC Quadrotor Thesis: What's Solved, What's Wrong, What's Worth Doing

## TL;DR

- **The two "least confident" ideas already have named prior art, but only in adjacent form — leaving narrow, genuine slices for you.** Dimensionless controller transfer is an established line (Girard's Buckingham-π policies, 2024; dimensionless MPC, 2025), and cross-vehicle transfer of adaptive/learning controllers is published (Chowdhary et al. ICRA 2013; Pereida–Schoellig RA-L 2018; Neural-Fly 2022). The unclaimed residue is specifically: dimensionless *MRAC weight vectors*, transferring a *populated concurrent-learning history stack*, attention *over* that stack, and treating waypoint spacing Δs as an experimental variable.
- **Your recalled citations are real and prove what you think they prove — but concurrent learning is normally used *alongside* robustness modifications, not instead of them, and the deadzone-halts-learning effect you measured is a known, expected pathology, not a bug.** Adopt integral concurrent learning (removes the state-derivative requirement), keep projection, and set the deadzone from the noise floor.
- **Classical MRAC is not the raw-performance leader in 2026 (INDI, NMPC, and learning-based controllers lead), so position the thesis in the live "MRAC + learned component" revival (Deep-MRAC, GP-MRAC, Neural-Fly) and defend it on interpretability, provable bounds, embedded cost, and certifiability — not on beating L1/INDI.**

## Executive summary — the five findings that most change the plan

1. **Cross-vehicle transfer of a learning-based adaptive controller is already published — by the concurrent-learning originators.** Chowdhary, Wu, Cutler & How, "Rapid Transfer of Controllers Between UAVs Using Learning-Based Adaptive Control," ICRA 2013 (DOI 10.1109/ICRA.2013.6631353), imports a baseline linear controller from one quadrotor onto a dynamically different one and uses concurrent-learning adaptive control to recover tracking, validated in MIT's RAVEN. This does not kill the thesis, but "transfer a controller between vehicles using concurrent learning" is prior art. The genuinely unclaimed variant is transferring the *populated history stack itself* — the targeted search found no paper that does this, and the closest one (Chowdhary 2013) re-collects the stack on the target vehicle.

2. **Dimensionless controller transfer is an active, named research line — you are not inventing it, you are joining it.** Girard's "Dimensionless Policies based on the Buckingham π Theorem" (arXiv 2307.15852; *Mathematics* 12(5):709, 2024) and Bakarji et al.'s "Dimensionally Consistent Learning with Buckingham Pi" establish exactly the idea of dividing out plant scales to obtain transferable, dimensionless control knowledge; there is even a dimensionless-MPC transfer paper (arXiv 2512.08667). The novelty is not "non-dimensionalise control parameters" — that exists — but "non-dimensionalise MRAC adaptive weights specifically," which appears unclaimed.

3. **Neural-Fly (Caltech, *Science Robotics* 2022) is extremely close to the instrumental claim and must be treated as the primary baseline.** It learns a shared basis offline via meta-learning (DAIML, using only 12 minutes of flight data gathered in six wind conditions), adapts linear mixing coefficients online with a composite adaptation law, has exponential-stability guarantees, runs onboard a "credit-card sized" Raspberry Pi 4 using the standard PX4 attitude controller, and *already demonstrates cross-drone transfer*. The thesis's "library of priors + select at runtime" is a discrete-selection cousin of Neural-Fly's continuous scheme; the closest robotics analogue to the prior-library idea is FAMLE (Kaushik, Anne & Mouret, 2020), which meta-trains several priors and selects the best at runtime.

4. **Concurrent learning solves the PE problem exactly as remembered — the citations are real — but it is normally used *alongside* robustness modifications, and it needs a history stack of at least N = 6 linearly independent points for your 6-term basis.** The guarantee requires the uncertainty to lie in the span of the basis (structured matching); under unmodelled dynamics the guarantee degrades to bounded (UUB) errors.

5. **The deadzone-halts-learning problem you measured is a known, named pathology, and your two-configuration fix is defensible but should be reframed.** The literature calls the underlying issue the robustness-vs-performance tradeoff of deadzone/σ/e-modifications; the modern answer is composite/concurrent learning, which *reduces* (does not eliminate) the need for a deadzone. Setting the deadzone from the noise floor is standard practice (the "relative deadzone").

## Q1. Concurrent learning and the rank condition

**Citations verified as real.** Chowdhary & Johnson, "Concurrent learning for convergence in adaptive control without persistency of excitation," 49th IEEE CDC 2010, pp. 3674–3679, DOI 10.1109/CDC.2010.5717148 (cited ~537×). The PhD thesis exists: Chowdhary, "Concurrent Learning for Convergence in Adaptive Control without Persistency of Excitation," Georgia Institute of Technology, 2010. The flight-test validation is Chowdhary & Johnson, "Theory and Flight-Test Validation of a Concurrent-Learning Adaptive Controller," *J. Guidance, Control, and Dynamics* 34(2):592–607, 2011, DOI 10.2514/1.46866.

**What it actually proves.** For an adaptive controller that uses recorded and instantaneous data concurrently, a verifiable condition on *linear independence of the recorded data* is sufficient to guarantee exponential convergence of both tracking error and parameter error. This condition (rank of the history stack = number of basis functions) is less restrictive and easier to monitor than persistent excitation. Your memory of the claim is accurate.

**Exact assumptions.** The guarantee is for uncertainty that lies in the span of the chosen basis — i.e., structured matching, Δ = −Θ*ᵀΦ(x) exactly. Under unmodelled dynamics (uncertainty not in the span), exponential convergence to the true weights is lost; you get uniform ultimate boundedness with a residual set governed by the representation error. The classical CL law also requires an estimate of the state derivative (to compute the recorded model error εⱼ), which is the main practical limitation.

**History-stack management.** The standard population/eviction algorithm is singular-value maximisation: Chowdhary & Johnson, "A Singular Value Maximizing Data Recording Algorithm for Concurrent Learning," ACC 2011. A candidate point replaces a stored one only if it increases the minimum singular value of the stack, driving the stack toward maximal richness. Cost is roughly O(N·p²) per update for a p-basis, N-point stack — trivial on a Cortex-M4 for p = 6.

**What superseded it (2012–2026).** *Integral Concurrent Learning* (Kamalapurkar/Parikh/Dixon, arXiv 1512.03464) removes the state-derivative requirement by using a finite-window integral of the dynamics — this is the single most important upgrade for your firmware because you avoid estimating angular acceleration. *Composite MRAC* (Lavretsky, "Combined/Composite Model Reference Adaptive Control," IEEE TAC 54(11):2692–2697, 2009) is the closely related direct+indirect idea. Exponentially convergent bounds: Chowdhary, Yucelen, Mühlegg & Johnson, *Int. J. Adaptive Control & Signal Processing* 27(4):280–301, 2013. Recent (2022–2026): Le & Teel, "Concurrent learning in high-order tuners for parameter identification," IEEE CDC 2022; hybrid/accelerated CL (Ochoa et al., L4DC 2021; "Prescribed-Time and Hyperexponential Concurrent Learning," arXiv 2502.11303); and sparsity-promoting integral CL (Satharasi et al., arXiv 2604.06338), which fuses SINDy-style basis selection with ICL and stability guarantees.

**Transfer of a history stack between vehicles.** The closest published work is Chowdhary, Wu, Cutler & How, ICRA 2013 (DOI 10.1109/ICRA.2013.6631353), which transfers the *baseline controller* and runs concurrent learning afresh on the target; it does not port the recorded (Φⱼ, εⱼ) pairs across airframes. So transferring a populated stack is unclaimed — but note it is arguably ill-posed, because a stack recorded on plant A has εⱼ scaled by plant A's dynamics; you would need to rescale it, which is exactly where your non-dimensionalisation idea becomes load-bearing.

**Practical minimum.** For a 6-basis problem you need at least 6 linearly independent stored points; in practice CL papers use 2–5× the basis dimension (roughly 15–30 points) for conditioning margin. At 6 floats each that is well under 1 kB — a non-issue on an STM32F4.

**Maturity: flown on hardware** (2011 flight tests; 2013 UAV transfer).

## Q2. Non-dimensionalisation of adaptive weights — precedent assessment

**This has precedent as a general idea but appears unclaimed for MRAC weights specifically.** The relevant body of work:

- **Buckingham-π for control policies:** Girard, "Dimensionless Policies based on the Buckingham π Theorem: Is This a Good Way to Generalize Numerical Results?" arXiv 2307.15852, published in *Mathematics* 12(5):709, 2024. Build directly on this — it shows that restating a motion-control problem in dimensionless variables (1) reduces the number of policy parameters and (2) lets a policy computed for one system transfer *exactly* to any dimensionally similar system. This is the rigorous version of your C_L analogy.
- **Buckingham-π + learning:** Bakarji, Callaham, Brunton & Kutz, "Dimensionally Consistent Learning with Buckingham Pi" (arXiv 2202.04643; *Nature Computational Science* 2022), including a "BuckiNet" and a SINDy-based variant to discover dimensionless groups from data.
- **Dimensionless MPC transfer:** "Direct transfer of optimized controllers to similar systems using dimensionless MPC," arXiv 2512.08667 — same philosophy, applied to MPC, demonstrated on cartpole and car racing.
- **Controller scaling (classical):** Zhiqiang Gao's "Scaling and Parameterization Based Controller Tuning" (frequency/gain scaling to a unit-gain–unit-bandwidth plant) is the classical precedent — reduce the plant to UGUB form, then scale a known controller. Your 1/K factoring is a special case.
- **Regressor normalisation:** In adaptive control, normalisation of the regressor (dividing Φ by 1+ΦᵀΦ or similar) is classically motivated by *numerical conditioning and global boundedness of the adaptive law*, not transfer (Ioannou & Sun, *Robust Adaptive Control*, 1996). It is introduced precisely to preserve boundedness, so the stability proof is preserved by construction; it slows convergence in the high-signal regime.

**Cross-plant parameter transfer under other names:** the strongest direct precedents are Pereida, Helwa & Schoellig, "Data-Efficient Multirobot, Multitask Transfer Learning for Trajectory Tracking," IEEE RA-L 3(2):1260–1267, 2018, DOI 10.1109/LRA.2018.2795653 (arXiv 1709.04543), which combines L1 adaptive control with iterative learning control and reports, verbatim, that "using information from tracking a single trajectory learned by one quadrotor reduces, on average, the first-iteration tracking error on another quadrotor by 74%" (two platforms, six trajectories); and the Chowdhary ICRA 2013 controller-transfer paper.

**Verdict:** The *mechanism* (divide out plant scales to transfer) is established; cite Girard rather than presenting it as invented. The *specific application to MRAC adaptive weights Θ using the 1/K matching-condition argument* is not something I found published. That is a genuine, if incremental, contribution — reframe it from "novel mechanism" to "first application of dimensionless-policy transfer to MRAC weight vectors."

**Maturity: theory + simulation** (Girard, dimensionless MPC); **flown** for the L1+ILC transfer (Pereida/Schoellig).

## Q3. Scenario-conditioned priors — closest prior art

The idea is a re-derivation of several established architectures; the question is which it most resembles and what remains unclaimed.

- **MMAC/MMAE and second-level adaptation (closest classical match).** Narendra & Balakrishnan, "Adaptive Control Using Multiple Models," IEEE TAC 42(2):171–187, 1997; Narendra & Han, "The changing face of adaptive control: the use of multiple models," *Annual Reviews in Control* 35(1):1–12, 2011; Han & Narendra, "New Concepts in Adaptive Control Using Multiple Models," IEEE TAC 57(1):78–89, 2012. A library of models/controllers indexed by operating condition, with switching or blending, is *exactly* the multiple-model paradigm. Your "σ-modification whose attractor is the prior" is a soft/blended version of this. Maturity: flown/experimental (e.g., twin-rotor MIMO).
- **Gain scheduling / LPV.** The classical answer to "different gains for different operating points." Your scheme is a learned, adaptive gain schedule.
- **GP-MRAC / Bayesian adaptive control (closest principled match).** Chowdhary, Kingravi, How & Vela, "Bayesian Nonparametric Adaptive Control Using Gaussian Processes," IEEE TNNLS 26(3):537–550, 2015; experimental validation Grande, Chowdhary & How, *J. Aerospace Information Systems* 11(9):565–578, 2014. A GP prior over the uncertainty is explicit and principled — the rigorous way to encode a "prior" and adapt from it. L1-GP (Gahlawat et al., L4DC 2020) is the L1 analogue.
- **Neural-Fly / meta-learning (closest modern match).** O'Connell, Shi, Shi, Azizzadenesheli, Anandkumar, Yue & Chung, "Neural-Fly enables rapid learning for agile flight in strong winds," *Science Robotics* 7(66):eabm6597, 2022, DOI 10.1126/scirobotics.abm6597 (arXiv 2205.06908). Learns a shared basis offline (DAIML meta-learning, 12 min of flight data across six wind conditions), adapts linear coefficients online via composite adaptation, exponential stability, onboard Raspberry Pi 4 with the standard PX4 attitude controller, and transfers across drones. Difference from the thesis: Neural-Fly adapts *coefficients over a continuous shared basis*; the thesis proposes a *discrete library of full weight vectors* with runtime selection. The discrete-prior-library-with-selection idea is precisely FAMLE (Kaushik, Anne & Mouret, "Fast Online Adaptation in Robotics through Meta-Learning Embeddings of Simulated Priors," arXiv 2003.04663) — meta-train several priors, select the most suitable at runtime, adapt with a few gradient steps. The thesis is essentially FAMLE-for-MRAC-weights.
- **L1 adaptive with pre-specified filters.** Hovakimyan & Cao, *L1 Adaptive Control Theory: Guaranteed Robustness with Fast Adaptation*, SIAM, 2010.

**Verdict:** Scenario-conditioned priors are a re-derivation of MMAC + meta-learned priors. Left unclaimed: (a) doing this *inside a classical MRAC weight space* with an explicit σ-modification-toward-prior term, and (b) making the priors dimensionless so the library is airframe-portable. Frame the contribution around (a)+(b), and benchmark explicitly against Neural-Fly and FAMLE.

## Q4. The robustness-versus-learning tension

**Your measurement reflects a known, named pathology.** The deadzone/σ/e-modifications trade regulation/convergence for robustness; several 2022–2026 sources say so plainly (e.g., the "μ-modification" paper, Sanner-lineage, *Systems & Control Letters* 2022; the deadzone-adapted disturbance-suppression paper, *SCL* 2024, which notes the deadzone alone gives only *local* robustness). The deadzone *suspends adaptation* whenever the error is inside the band, so with a well-tuned baseline the error is inside the band almost immediately — exactly your 0.2 s observation. This is expected, not a bug. Note also the "bursting" phenomenon (Ioannou & Sun): without a sufficient deadzone, indefinite cycling between parameter-error growth and tracking-error peaks can prevent convergence — so removing the deadzone entirely is not safe.

- **Two-configuration split (permissive sim / conservative flight):** not a formally named method, but close to established practice under names like "metrics-driven adaptive control" (Nguyen, NASA), where adaptation aggressiveness is scheduled against a robustness metric, and to curriculum/training-vs-deployment splits in learning-based control. Defensible, but you must argue it does not invalidate the flight-configuration stability guarantee — the sim config is for *learning the priors*, the flight config for *deploying* them.
- **Does CL replace the modifications?** No. The literature presents concurrent/composite learning as the modern *complement* to σ/e-mod that reduces reliance on them, not a wholesale replacement. Projection is almost always retained for hard parameter bounds. Recommendation: keep projection, keep a small noise-floor deadzone, replace σ/e-mod drift-prevention with CL.
- **Deadzone from the noise floor:** yes — the "relative deadzone" sets the band from the disturbance/noise bound rather than by tuning. Set e_deadzone ≈ k·σ_noise where σ_noise is the measured gyro-noise RMS and k ∈ [2, 3].

**Maturity: theory + flight-relevant** (NASA metrics-driven work; deadzone theory is mature).

## Q5. Regressor basis selection

- **SINDy for adaptive-control regressors — yes, and with stability guarantees (very recent).** Satharasi, Ogri, Qureshi & Kamalapurkar, "Adaptive Control with Sparse Identification of Nonlinear Dynamics," arXiv 2604.06338, presents sparsity-promoting integral concurrent learning (SP-ICL) that selects active basis functions online *and* provides adaptive-control stability/convergence guarantees. Also "Adaptive SINDy: Residual Force System Identification Based UAV Disturbance Rejection," arXiv 2603.08863 (SINDy + RLS for quadrotor wind rejection), and SINDy-MPC with Lyapunov guarantees for a VTOL UAV. This directly serves your "test alternative bases" aim and is 2026-fresh. Original method: Brunton, Proctor & Kutz, "Discovering governing equations from data by sparse identification of nonlinear dynamical systems," *PNAS* 113(15):3932–3937, 2016, plus SINDYc (with control).
- **Structured vs RBF/universal approximators.** Consensus: structured/physics bases give better extrapolation and far smaller stacks; RBF/GP give semi-global coverage but blow up the rank requirement. GP-MRAC (Chowdhary et al. 2015) was motivated precisely by the failure of fixed-center RBFNs outside the training domain. For CL specifically, an N-RBF basis needs ≥N independent recorded points — a large unstructured basis makes the rank condition expensive, which argues *for* your compact 6-term physics basis.
- **Per-regime basis switching:** essentially multiple-model/TS-fuzzy territory (Q3/Q6). I found no clean quadrotor paper that switches the *adaptive regressor basis* per flight regime with a stability proof — a plausible small contribution, but the risk is loss of the common Lyapunov function across switches (bursting).
- **RBF center placement / normalised state for transfer:** centers are usually gridded or placed by k-means over the expected operating domain; GP-MRAC removes preallocation. Normalising the state so centers transfer between vehicles is exactly the dimensionless-transfer idea (Q2) and is not standard — another place your contribution lands.

## Q6. Attention ≡ Takagi–Sugeno ≡ LPV, and attention over a history stack

- **The equivalences are published, but piecemeal, not as a single control-theoretic statement.** Softmax attention = Nadaraya–Watson kernel regression with a Gaussian/RBF kernel *when queries and keys have unit norm* is stated explicitly in multiple 2023–2026 sources (e.g., "Can a Transformer Represent a Kalman Filter?", arXiv 2312.06937; the d2l.ai textbook derivation; and recent kernel-attention papers). TS-fuzzy = polytopic quasi-LPV, with a shared LMI/common-quadratic-Lyapunov machinery, is established: Rotondo, Puig, Nejjari & Witczak, "Automated generation and comparison of Takagi–Sugeno and polytopic quasi-LPV models," *Fuzzy Sets and Systems* 277:44–64, 2015; foundational stability is Tanaka & Sugeno via parallel distributed compensation and a common P matrix. What I did *not* find is a single paper stating the full chain "softmax attention ≡ TS blend ≡ LPV gain schedule" as a control result — so your internal derivation, if written carefully, is a genuine expository/unifying contribution, but each individual link is prior art and must be cited as such.
- **Does TS/LPV stability carry over to softmax blending?** Partially, and with real caveats. The common-quadratic-Lyapunov / one-LMI-per-local-model route applies to *fixed, designed* membership functions. Attention breaks two assumptions: (1) the "centers" (keys) are *learned*, not designed, so the membership partition is data-dependent; (2) continuous-time TS stability with state-dependent premises requires bounding the *time-derivatives of the membership functions*, which for a softmax over a moving query is nontrivial — the TS overview literature explicitly flags this as the hard part (fuzzy Lyapunov functions, local results, conservatism). So "a stability proof route already exists" is too strong; a route exists *if* you freeze the keys/centers and bound membership-function variation. State that as an explicit assumption.
- **Attention to query a concurrent-learning history stack:** I found no paper doing this. Plausibly novel. Nearest neighbors are "Temporal Attention for Adaptive Control of Euler–Lagrange Systems with Unobservable Memory" (arXiv 2605.06877) and transformer-based online adaptation for quadrotor fault tolerance (arXiv 2505.08223) — related but not the same construct. This is your most defensible architectural novelty, though its practical value over singular-value-maximisation stack management is unproven and must be demonstrated, not asserted.
- **Attention/transformer flight control on embedded hardware, 2024–2026:** Bhattacharya, Rao, Parikh, Kunapuli, Wu, Tao, Matni & Kumar, "Vision Transformers for End-to-End Vision-Based Quadrotor Obstacle Avoidance," arXiv 2405.10391 (ICRA 2025) — first ViT for end-to-end quadrotor control, flown to 7 m/s; and the RL fault-tolerant transformer-adaptation work (arXiv 2505.08223). These are perception/policy transformers, not inner-loop rate controllers — the embedded inner-loop attention controller space is still open.

## Q7. Dense trajectory tracking — is the metric set defensible?

- **Your metric set is conventional and largely complete.** Position RMSE is the field-standard headline metric (used in essentially every paper found: the NMPC-vs-DFBC comparison arXiv 2109.01365, Neural-Fly, RAPTOR, etc.). Cross-track error and along-track lag are the standard *path-following* decomposition (see "Path-Flyer: A Benchmark of Quadrotor Path Following Algorithms," which uses mean distance-to-path). Adding these two to RMSE is good practice. Also report max error and control effort/energy (common in 2025 comparisons) and, for adaptive control, the *transient* error over the first seconds.
- **Reference density / Δs as an experimental variable — genuinely underexplored.** Extensive work sweeps *speed* (m/s) and *aggressiveness*, but I found no quadrotor tracking paper that treats *waypoint spacing / reference quantization* as a first-class independent variable. This looks like a real, if narrow, novelty. Caveat: it is closely related to minimum-snap trajectory smoothness and sampling-rate effects, so position it carefully.
- **Numbers to compare against (real hardware).** Neural-Fly: verbatim, "a mean tracking error of 2.9 cm in 0 km/h wind" and "an average improvement of 66% over a nonlinear tracking controller, 42% over an ℒ1 adaptive controller, and 35% over an incremental nonlinear dynamics inversion (INDI) controller," in winds "up to 43.6 kilometers/hour (12.1 meters/second)." NMPC vs DFBC (Sun, Romero, Foehn, Kaufmann & Scaramuzza, IEEE T-RO 2022, arXiv 2109.01365): verbatim, "For NMPC, we see more than 364% and 115% increase in position and heading RMSE if INDI is not used. For DFBC, removing INDI leads to a more severe consequence: more than 705% and 492% of position and heading RMSE increase"; NMPC+INDI position RMSE was 0.102 m vs 0.343 m for NMPC+PID (70% lower), at speeds up to 72 km/h — strong evidence your inner-loop focus matters. L1-augmented geometric control (arXiv 2109.06998): ~5× smaller tracking error than geometric alone. RAPTOR (Eschmann, Albani & Loianno, arXiv 2509.11481; also *Science Robotics*, DOI 10.1126/scirobotics.aec1481): a "tiny 2084-parameter neural policy that adapts zero-shot to 10 unseen real quadrotors via in-context learning," achieving, verbatim, "competitive Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) values (e.g., 0.19 m for a 5.5s trajectory on Crazyflie) comparable to policies specifically trained for single quadrotors," across vehicles from 32 g to 2.4 kg. These are your comparison anchors.
- **Standard trajectory sets:** circle, lemniscate/figure-eight, and aggressive/racing trajectories are the de facto standards; adopt the lemniscate with published baselines for comparability.

## Q8. Where does MRAC sit in 2026?

**Honest assessment: classical MRAC is not the raw-performance leader for quadrotor inner loops, but it is not obsolete, and the "MRAC + learned component" revival is exactly where this thesis should live.**

- **What leads on performance:** INDI (Tal & Karaman, "Accurate Tracking of Aggressive Quadrotor Trajectories Using Incremental Nonlinear Dynamic Inversion and Differential Flatness," IEEE TCST 29(3):1203–1218, 2021) and NMPC/DFBC with INDI inner loops dominate agile tracking (arXiv 2109.01365); adaptive NMPC (Hanover, Foehn, Sun, Kaufmann & Scaramuzza, IEEE RA-L 7(2):690–697, 2022) handles unknown payloads; learning-based methods (Neural-Fly, NeuroBEM-backed models, RL foundation policies like RAPTOR) lead in disturbance rejection and cross-platform generality.
- **Fair head-to-heads exist and generally do not favor plain MRAC:** Neural-Fly beats L1 by 42% and INDI by 35%; the RSS 2025 "Leveling the Playing Field" (Kunapuli, Welde, Jayaraman & Kumar, arXiv 2506.17832) carefully compares RL vs geometric control; a 2025 *Soft Computing* paper compares adaptive vs classical controllers. Plain MRAC rarely wins.
- **The honest remaining argument for MRAC:** interpretability (a 6-term physical regressor is human-readable), provable Lyapunov/UUB bounds, tiny embedded cost (runs at 200 Hz on an M4 where NMPC cannot), and certifiability (there is even formal-verification work: "Verification of L1 Adaptive Control using Verse," arXiv 2303.13819). State this explicitly rather than claiming performance parity.
- **The revival that is your closest relative:** Deep-MRAC (Joshi & Chowdhary, arXiv 1909.08602) and GP-MRAC combine MRAC with learned features; Neural-Fly is composite-adaptation MRAC with a meta-learned basis. This is the live, publishable frontier — MRAC as the *stable online layer* under a learned/meta-learned representation. Position the thesis here, not as "MRAC vs the world."

## Q9. Public quadrotor flight datasets

- **NeuroBEM (ETH/UZH):** Bauersfeld, Kaufmann, Foehn, Sun & Scaramuzza, RSS 2021. ~1 h 15 min of agile flight, Vicon ground truth at 400 Hz, includes rotor-speed/motor data and full state — **usable for identifying (K, p, T)**. Best single source for aggressive dynamics. Research-use licence; available at rpg.ifi.uzh.ch/neuro_bem.
- **Blackbird (MIT):** Antonini, Guerra, Murali, Sayre-McCord & Karaman, *IJRR* 2020. 18 trajectories, 0.5–9 m/s, onboard rotor-speed measurements + IMU + mocap, ~10 h. Includes motor/rotor data — usable for ID.
- **UZH-FPV:** Delmerico, Cieslewski, Rebecq, Faessler & Scaramuzza, ICRA 2019. Aggressive FPV racing, IMU + events + Leica ground truth — **but oriented to visual-inertial odometry; actuator/motor-command logging is limited**, so less directly useful for (K,p,T) than NeuroBEM/Blackbird. Verify actuator signals before relying on it.
- **EuRoC MAV:** classic but ~2 kg VIO dataset, no actuator focus.
- **PX4/Betaflight blackbox corpora:** these exist in the wild (Flight Review logs, public .ulg/.bbl files) and *do* contain rate setpoints and motor outputs — exactly enough for (K,p,T) ID — but they are heterogeneous, uncurated, and licence status varies per uploader. Treat as a bonus, not a backbone.
- **NanoBench (2025):** a newer nano-quadrotor (Crazyflie-class, ~27 g) multi-task benchmark explicitly for system ID, control, and state estimation — worth checking as it is purpose-built for your task.
- **Cross-vehicle system ID from such corpora:** Eschmann, Albani & Loianno, "Data-Driven System Identification of Quadrotors Subject to Motor Delays," arXiv 2404.07837, is directly relevant precedent for identifying per-airframe dynamics (including delay) from logs.

Your plan — identify (K, p, T) per airframe from rate + motor data, then compute ideal weights analytically — is sound, and NeuroBEM + Blackbird are the right backbone.

## Q10. Transport delay and adaptation aggressiveness

**Your instinct is correct and well-supported: delay upper-bounds the usable adaptation rate.** The canonical result is that for standard MRAC the time-delay margin → 0 as the adaptation gain Γ → ∞ (Cao, Patel, Konda Reddy, Hovakimyan, Lavretsky & Wise, "Are phase and time-delay margins always adversely affected by high-gain?"). Concretely, a University of Minnesota thesis ("Time Delay Margin Analysis for Adaptive Flight Control Laws") shows a hard design constraint: above a critical Γ the minimum time-delay-margin requirement is violated — "it is impossible to design a controller that both benefits from adaptation and satisfies the robustness requirement." So dropping your 15 ms delay in sim and letting Γ grow gives a falsely stable plant, exactly as you found.

- **Standard way to compute max safe Γ from identified delay:** the NASA work (Nguyen et al., "On Time Delay Margin Estimation for Adaptive Control," NTRS 20110016044) gives three analytical methods — Padé approximation, Lyapunov–Krasovskii, and the matrix-measure method — to estimate the time-delay margin as a function of Γ and the modification (σ, e, optimal-control-modification). Use the matrix-measure method (least conservative) to invert "required TDM ≥ your 15 ms" into a Γ_max. L1 adaptive control is the architecture that decouples Γ from the delay margin (margin bounded away from zero as Γ → ∞), which is why L1 is attractive if you need aggressive adaptation under delay.

**Maturity: theory + flight-validated** (NASA GTM).

## Q11. Sim-to-real transfer for control parameters

- **Domain randomization for controller *parameters* (not just RL policies):** the dominant practice is to randomize mass, inertia, thrust, motor delay, and drag over an ensemble and tune a single robust parameter set. "One Net to Rule Them All: Domain Randomization in Quadcopter Racing Across Different Platforms," arXiv 2504.21586, is the key recent datapoint: a single domain-randomized controller flies both 3-inch and 5-inch quadrotors — but *increasing randomization improves robustness while reducing speed*, and the authors found online adaptation did not add the hoped-for gains. This is the central tradeoff: randomization buys invariance at the cost of performance (the "conservative robust controller" problem is also documented in arXiv 2605.16015, where a domain-randomized controller had the *worst* nominal-condition RMSE, 0.111 m, due to high-frequency oscillation).
- **Ensemble-of-airframes for vehicle-invariant parameters:** established — Mordatch et al. and Lowrey et al. showed ensemble/model-randomized policies transfer better than single-model ones (reviewed in "Robot Learning from Randomized Simulations," arXiv 2111.00956). Molchanov et al., "Sim-to-(multi)-real: Transfer of Low-Level Robust Control Policies to Multiple Quadrotors," IROS 2019, transfers low-level quadrotor control across multiple real vehicles.
- **What it buys you:** robustness/invariance, at a measurable performance cost. This is precisely the argument *for* your prior-selection scheme over pure domain randomization: rather than one conservative parameter set for all airframes, keep per-scenario priors and select — recovering the performance blanket randomization sacrifices. Frame your instrumental claim as the adaptive alternative to conservative domain randomization, and cite arXiv 2504.21586 as the baseline you improve on.

## Claims in the brief we could not verify or found contradicted

1. **"We have found no precedent for transferring adaptive control between vehicles."** *Contradicted.* Chowdhary, Wu, Cutler & How (ICRA 2013) and Pereida, Helwa & Schoellig (RA-L 2018) both do cross-vehicle transfer of learning-based/adaptive controllers. The unclaimed residue is narrower: transferring the *dimensionless MRAC weight vector* and/or the *populated history stack* specifically.
2. **"Non-dimensionalisation of adaptive weights is the decision we are least confident has precedent."** *Partially contradicted.* Dimensionless control-policy transfer is an established, named line (Girard 2024; Bakarji et al.; dimensionless MPC). The MRAC-weight-specific application is what is unclaimed.
3. **"TS/LPV stability machinery applies directly to a softmax-blended controller, so a stability proof route already exists."** *Overstated / unverified as stated.* The machinery applies to fixed, designed membership functions; with learned keys and a moving softmax query you must additionally bound membership-function time-derivatives. No single paper establishes the full attention ≡ TS ≡ LPV chain as a control result.
4. **"Concurrent learning removes the drift that motivated the deadzone."** *Partially unverified.* CL/composite methods *reduce* reliance on drift-prevention modifications but the literature uses them alongside projection; none claim the deadzone is fully removable in the presence of measurement noise (and the bursting phenomenon argues against full removal).
5. **"Δs (waypoint spacing) as a first-class experimental variable."** *No literature found either way* — speed and aggressiveness sweeps are common, but no waypoint-quantization sweep. Genuine white space, stated as such.
6. **Attention over a concurrent-learning history stack.** *No literature found.* Appears novel.

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 — Reframe two claims before writing (do this first).**
- Recast non-dimensionalisation from "invented mechanism" to "first application of Buckingham-π dimensionless-policy transfer (Girard 2024) to MRAC adaptive weights." Cite Girard, Bakarji, and dimensionless MPC up front.
- Recast the prior library from "new idea" to "FAMLE-style discrete meta-learned priors realized in MRAC weight space," and adopt Neural-Fly and FAMLE as your two named baselines.

**Stage 2 — Fix the controller before running experiments.**
- Adopt **integral concurrent learning** (arXiv 1512.03464), not classical CL, to avoid needing angular-acceleration estimates on the STM32F4.
- Keep projection; replace σ/e-mod drift-prevention with ICL; set the deadzone from the measured gyro-noise floor (relative deadzone, e_deadzone ≈ 2–3·σ_noise) rather than the fixed 0.05.
- Compute Γ_max from the identified 15 ms delay using the NASA matrix-measure method (NTRS 20110016044) before any aggressive-learning experiment. Threshold: if the time-delay margin at your chosen Γ drops below ~2× the identified delay (i.e., below ~30 ms), reduce Γ.

**Stage 3 — Experiments that establish novelty.**
- Make Δs the headline independent variable (clearest white space) and report RMSE + cross-track + along-track lag + transient error vs Δs.
- Demonstrate dimensionless prior transfer on ≥3 airframes identified from **NeuroBEM and Blackbird** logs; this is the cheap multi-airframe evidence and directly answers whether the C_L analogy holds.
- If you pursue attention-over-stack, treat it as a controlled comparison against singular-value-maximisation stack management, not a standalone claim.

**Stage 4 — Benchmarks and thresholds.**
- Compare against Neural-Fly's 2.9 cm still-air RMSE and its 35–42% margins over INDI/L1, and RAPTOR's 0.19 m 5.5 s figure-eight RMSE (on a Crazyflie), on comparable trajectories.
- Decision thresholds that change the plan: (a) if plain MRAC cannot get within ~2× of L1/INDI tracking on the lemniscate, pivot the thesis argument to interpretability/embedded-cost/certifiability rather than performance; (b) if dimensionless transfer does not reduce cross-airframe first-flight error by a margin comparable to Pereida/Schoellig's 74%, demote the airframe-invariance "bonus" claim to a negative result (still publishable).

## Caveats

- Several close-match findings come from arXiv preprints dated 2025–2026 (e.g., SP-ICL arXiv 2604.06338, Adaptive SINDy arXiv 2603.08863, RAPTOR arXiv 2509.11481, "One Net" arXiv 2504.21586, dimensionless MPC arXiv 2512.08667); these are not all peer-reviewed and dates/venues should be re-checked at submission.
- The RAPTOR figure-eight RMSE (0.19 m, Crazyflie) and the "10 unseen real quadrotors" count supersede earlier informal figures; use the enriched values.
- The claim that transferring a *populated history stack* between vehicles is unclaimed rests on absence of evidence; the Chowdhary 2013 paper is close enough that the full PDF (MIT DSpace 1721.1/96961) should be read to confirm it does not port stack contents.
- The TS/LPV ↔ attention stability transfer is nuanced; do not assert a proof exists until the membership-function-derivative bounding is done and the keys/centers are frozen.
- Neural-Fly's cross-drone transfer used a representation trained on an Intel Aero drone (1.4 kg) and deployed on a custom 2.6 kg vehicle; the paper states, verbatim, that "Neural-Fly outperforms Neural-Fly-Transfer slightly" while both "outperform all other methods in all tests." Treat the transfer as demonstrated but with a small, quantified performance cost when quoting it.
